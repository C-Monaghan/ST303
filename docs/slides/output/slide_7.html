Let’s start with a scatter plot

Question: What do you notice about the relationship between (X) and (Y)?

Describing a relationship

When examining a scatterplot, we typically consider three things

Direction

  • Positive: larger values of (X) tend to accompany larger values of (Y)
  • Negative: larger values of (X) tend to accompany smaller values of (Y)

Strength

  • Strong: observations follow a relatively clear pattern
  • Weak: observations are more dispersed around the overall pattern

Form

  • Linear: the relationship follows an approximately straight-line pattern.
  • Nonlinear: the relationship follows a curve or another pattern.


The correlation coefficient

The sample correlation coefficient measures the strength and direction of the linear association between two quantitative variables

\[ r = \frac{S_{xy}}{\sqrt{S_{xx} \times S_{yy}}} \]

  • Bounded: \(-1 \leq r \leq 1\)
  • Direction:
    • \(r > 0\) indicates positive linear association
    • \(r < 0\) indicates negative linear association
  • Strength: Values closer to \(-1\) or \(1\) indicate stronger linear association.
  • Symmetry: \(\operatorname{cor}(X,Y)=\operatorname{cor}(Y,X)\)
  • Unit-free: Correlation is unaffected by changing the measurement units

A correlation of zero means there is no linear association

It does not rule out a nonlinear relationship


  • The relationship is clearly nonlinear, even though the Pearson correlation can be close to zero.
  • A near-zero correlation does not necessarily mean that the variables are unrelated.
  • Takeaway: always inspect the scatterplot before interpreting a correlation coefficient.

Correlation and \(R^2\)

Recall that the formula for \(R^2\) is

\[ R^2 = \frac{SS_R}{SS_T} \qquad \text{OR} \qquad R^2 = 1 - \frac{SS_E}{SS_T} \]

It describes the proportion of the observed variation in \(Y\) accounted for by the fitted linear model

\[ R^2 = r^2 \] For example, if we got \(r = 0.8\), then we can say that \(R^2 = 0.64\)

The fitted linear model accounts for 64% of the variation in the response around its mean

Two datasets have correlations of 0.8 and −0.8.

Do they have the same \(R^2\)?

Correlation and \(R^2\)

Word of caution

Two \(R^2\) values may be similar even when the relationships have different structures

A high \(R^2\) does not guarantee that a linear model is appropriate

Always consider the scatterplot and relevant model assumptions

Regression towards the mean

What is it

  • Suppose a father is substantially taller than the population average
  • His son is expected to be taller than average too, because heights are positively associated
  • However, the predicted son’s height is typically closer to the population mean than the father’s height is, when both are measured on the same scale and the fitted slope is between zero and one

This is regression towards the mean

This helps explain why extreme observations on one variable tend to be associated with less extreme expected values of the other variable
